📜 The rules of the game

Ten rules that govern the whole method. Each step then has two or three of its own, behind the 📜 chip inside that step.

  1. Everything is built on the sampling distribution. Not the individual scores and not the population — the distribution of the STATISTIC across repeated samples. It is the only thing that says how much a result moves by chance.
  2. Three facts, every time: shape, centre, spread. Approximately Normal for large n; centred on the parameter, so the statistic is unbiased; and a spread with a formula. Those three facts are what every interval and test consume.
  3. Spread is where the √n lives. σ ⁄ √n for a mean, √(p(1−p)/n) for a proportion. Four times the data halves the uncertainty — never forget it is the ROOT of n.
  4. Every interval is estimate ± margin of error. And the margin is always z* × standard error. Learn the template once and only the standard error changes from case to case.
  5. The confidence level belongs to the METHOD. C is how often intervals built this way capture the truth in repeated samples. Once your interval is computed it either contains the parameter or it does not.
  6. z* is a lookup: 1.645, 1.96, 2.576. 90%, 95%, 99%. Split the leftover area in two and read the table — z* depends on the confidence level and on nothing else, least of all n.
  7. The test uses the NULL’s standard error; the interval uses the data’s. z = (p̂ − p₀) ⁄ √(p₀(1−p₀)/n) for a test, p̂ ± z*√(p̂(1−p̂)/n) for an interval. The two formulas look almost identical and are not interchangeable.
  8. Check the counts, not the sample size. Successes and failures both at least 10 — for every interval, and for the one-proportion test (there with np₀ and n(1−p₀)). The two-sample TEST is the exception: the deck asks only for at least 5 in each of the four cells. A sample of 400 with 6 successes fails, and plus-four is the repair.
  9. For two groups, variances add. Square, add, root — in that order. And for an INTERVAL each group keeps its own p̂; pooling belongs to the test, because only the test assumes p₁ = p₂.
  10. A P-value is a probability about DATA. It is computed inside the null world and never attaches to the hypothesis. “Fail to reject” means this sample was not strange enough, not that H₀ is true.

🏛️ The whole method in one paragraph

Everything starts from the sampling distribution: the distribution of a statistic across repeated samples, described by three facts — it is approximately Normal for large n, it is centred on the parameter, and its spread has a formula. For a mean that spread is σ ⁄ √n; for a proportion it is √(p(1−p)/n), with no separate σ because p decides it. From those three facts both procedures follow. An interval is estimate ± z* × standard error, where z* is a lookup — 1.645, 1.96, 2.576 — and the confidence level describes the method rather than your one interval: for a single proportion that is p̂ ± z*√(p̂(1−p̂)/n), valid when successes and failures both reach 10, with plus-four improving the true confidence level generally and mattering most when the counts are small. A test goes the other way: fix a claimed p₀, build the sampling distribution that would hold if it were true — so the standard error uses p₀, not p̂ — and measure how far your result landed, reporting a P-value that is a probability about DATA in the null world. Run the margin-of-error formula backwards and it chooses your sample size, n = (z*/m)² p*(1−p*), with p* = 0.5 as the safe guess and always rounded up. Then the whole machine runs again for two groups: the statistic is p̂₁ − p̂₂, its variance is the sum of the two variances, and the interval keeps each group’s own p̂ — while the test, which assumes p₁ = p₂, pools all the successes over all the individuals instead. Finally, the same two proportions can be reported as a difference, whose null value is 0, or as a relative risk, whose null value is 1.

🔭 The sky test — the logic of the whole lecture

A deliberately silly question that contains the entire logic of hypothesis testing.

⬇ builds onnothing — this is where the chapter starts⬆ hands upthe one idea every formula in this lecture serves
  • ▸You cannot check a claim by looking at one thing. You list the outcomes the claim makes likely, then see whether what you got is on the list.
  • ▸H₀ is “there is no difference” — both groups equal, or the mean equal to 0. It is the claim you put on trial.
  • ▸Which distribution tests a hypothesis? Not the individual sample scores, and not the population scores — the distribution of SAMPLE MEANS.
🪝 memory hookThe sky test. You do not prove “blue” — you list the colours a blue sky is allowed to be, look up, and see whether today’s colour is on the list.

In words

The lecture opens with “the sky is blue — is that true?”, and the point is that you cannot check a claim by looking at one thing. You decide in advance which colours the claim makes likely and which would be a surprise, then you look up. That is a significance test. And the distribution you judge against is not the individual scores and not the population — it is the distribution of sample means.

  • Everything in this lecture is built on that sampling distribution. The two halves of the chapter just change what statistic it describes.
  • That is why the curve runs from −4 to +4: it is measured in standard errors, so any statistic lands on the same picture.

Rules of the game for step 1

  • Work inside the null world. Every test asks “how strange would my result be if H₀ were true?” — never “how likely is H₀?”
  • The sampling distribution is the ruler. Individual scores describe your data and the population is what you want to learn about; only the sampling distribution says how much a statistic moves by chance.
  • A statistic measured in standard errors is comparable to any other. That is what lets one bell curve serve means, proportions and differences alike.

Say it to a friend

“I start by assuming there’s nothing going on. Then I work out what my result would probably look like in that world. If what I actually got would be really unusual in that world, I stop believing the assumption.”

Key insight

Estimation and testing are the same machinery pointed in opposite directions. An interval starts from the data and reaches out for the parameter; a test starts from a claimed parameter and asks how far the data landed from it.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Treating a P-value as the probability that H₀ is true. It is a probability about DATA, computed in a world where H₀ is assumed true. The hypothesis never gets a probability.

Check yourself

Why can’t you test a hypothesis against the distribution of individual scores?
Because individual scores are far more variable than averages, so a result that is ordinary for one person can be extraordinary for a mean of sixteen. The test is about how much the STATISTIC moves by chance, and only the sampling distribution describes that.
Your friend says “p = .03 means there’s only a 3% chance the null is true.” Correct them.
“p = .03 means that IF the null were true, results at least this extreme would turn up in 3% of samples.” It is a probability about the data under an assumption, not about the assumption. The null is either true or it is not; it has no probability.

🎯 How much does a sample mean bounce?

Shape, centre and spread — the three facts that make estimation possible.

⬇ builds onthe idea that the sampling distribution is the ruler⬆ hands upσ ⁄ √n — a number for how far off your estimate probably is
  • ▸SHAPE. Since the population is Normal, so is the sampling distribution of x̄.
  • ▸CENTRE. The mean of the sampling distribution is the population mean µ — x̄ is an unbiased estimator.
  • ▸SPREAD. σx̄ = σ ⁄ √n = 20 ⁄ √16 = 5, the lecture’s own figure.
🪝 memory hookAveraging is noise-cancelling. Individual people are loud and disagree; their average is quiet. Quadruple the crowd and the remaining noise halves.

In words

Three facts, and they are the same three facts for every statistic in this course. SHAPE: if the population is Normal, so is the sampling distribution of x̄. CENTRE: its mean is the population mean µ, so x̄ is unbiased. SPREAD: σ ⁄ √n, which for the lecture’s numbers is 20 ⁄ √16 = 5. That third fact is the one that turns a single sample into a statement about a population.

  • That is where z comes from: z = (X − µ) ⁄ σ measures how many standard deviations from the mean a value sits.
  • And it builds the interval: with x̄ = 240.79, µ is within about 10 points either side in roughly 95% of samples — 230.79 to 250.79.

Rules of the game for step 2

  • It is the square ROOT of n, not n. Four times the sample halves the standard error; a hundred times the sample divides it by ten.
  • σ ⁄ √n is the spread of the SAMPLE MEAN, never of the individual scores. The individuals still have spread σ.
  • Unbiased means right ON AVERAGE across many samples. Your one sample mean is almost certainly not equal to µ.

Say it to a friend

“Individual scores are all over the place, but averages of 16 people are much steadier. That steadiness has a formula — the population spread divided by the square root of the sample size — and it’s what lets me say how close my average probably is to the truth.”

Key insight

The √n is why precision is expensive. Going from a margin of 10 to a margin of 5 does not cost twice the sample — it costs four times, and the rest of the lecture keeps running into that wall.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Dividing by n instead of √n. With σ = 20 and n = 16 that gives 1.25 instead of 5 — a standard error four times too small, and an interval four times too confident.

Check yourself

A population has σ = 30 and you take samples of 25. What is the standard error of the mean, and what would it be at n = 100?
30 ⁄ √25 = 6. At n = 100 it is 30 ⁄ 10 = 3. Quadrupling the sample halved the standard error — the √n at work, and the reason precision gets expensive.
Why is it the spread of the sampling distribution, and not σ itself, that goes into a confidence interval?
Because the interval is a statement about where µ sits relative to YOUR SAMPLE MEAN, and the sample mean is far steadier than an individual score. Using σ would describe how much one person varies, which is not the question.

🧱 Estimate ± margin of error

The shape every confidence interval in this course takes — and what the level really means.

⬇ builds ona standard error from step 2⬆ hands upthe template every interval in this lecture fills in
  • ▸Two parts, always: an interval of the form estimate ± margin of error, and a confidence level C.
  • ▸C is a property of the METHOD, not of your one interval — how often intervals built this way capture the truth in repeated samples.
  • ▸m = z* σ ⁄ √n for a mean. Higher confidence needs a bigger z*, so a 99% interval is always wider than a 95% one.
🪝 memory hookA confidence interval is a net, not a fish. The confidence level describes how often the net catches; it says nothing about whether this particular throw did.

In words

Every confidence interval has exactly two parts: an interval of the form estimate ± margin of error, and a confidence level C. The level is the part students get wrong. C is a property of the METHOD across repeated samples — how often intervals built this way capture the truth — not a probability about the one interval you computed.

  • We usually choose 90% or higher, because we want to be relatively sure of our conclusions. 95% is the most common.
  • What you may NOT say: “there is a 95% probability that µ is in this interval”. Once computed, it either contains µ or it does not.

Rules of the game for step 3

  • Write the level and the interval together. “90% confident that the interval from … to … captures …” is the sentence that earns the mark.
  • The probability lives in the procedure, before you look. After you compute, there is nothing left to be probable about.
  • Wider is not better. A 99.99% interval is nearly always true and nearly always useless; the level you pick is a trade, not a free improvement.

Say it to a friend

“I take my estimate and stretch a margin either side of it. The 95% doesn’t mean the truth is 95% likely to be in my range — it means if I kept doing this, 95% of the ranges I build would contain the truth.”

Key insight

Only two things can narrow an interval: a lower confidence level, or a bigger sample. The first buys precision by accepting more misses; the second buys it with money. There is no third option.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Saying “there is a 95% probability that µ lies between 230.79 and 250.79”. Those are fixed numbers and µ is a fixed number — the statement is either true or false, and the 95% belongs to the method that produced it.

A 95% interval for a mean comes out as (12.4, 18.6). Write the one-sentence interpretation, correctly.
“We are 95% confident that the interval from 12.4 to 18.6 captures the true population mean.” The confidence attaches to the interval-building method, not to the numbers — and the sentence has to name the population, not the sample.
You rebuild the same interval at 99% instead of 95%. What happens, and why?
It gets wider, because z* rises from 1.96 to 2.576. Being right more often, with the same data, is only possible by claiming less precisely. The only way to be both more confident and equally precise is to collect more data.

📏 Where z* comes from

A level C interval keeps area C in the middle of the standard Normal curve.

⬇ builds onthe estimate ± margin template⬆ hands upthe multiplier — 1.645, 1.96 or 2.576, for almost every question you will meet
  • ▸The definition: z* is the critical value for the standard Normal curve with area C between −z* and +z*.
  • ▸Learn three by heart — 90% → 1.645, 95% → 1.96, 99% → 2.576. They cover almost every exam question.
  • ▸Finding one by hand: for 80% you leave out 20%, so 10% in each tail; Table A gives z = −1.28 for area 0.1, so z* = 1.28.
🪝 memory hook1.645 — 1.96 — 2.576, said as a rhythm for 90, 95, 99. If you can recite those three, most interval questions become one multiplication.

In words

z* is the critical value for the standard Normal curve with area C between −z* and +z*. For 95% the 68-95-99.7 rule gives roughly 2, and the table sharpens it to 1.96. Finding one by hand is a lookup: for an 80% interval you leave 20% out, 10% in each tail, and search Table A for the point with area 0.1 to its left — z = −1.28, so z* = 1.28.

  • The quick version: for 95%, the 68-95-99.7 rule gives about 2, and the table sharpens it to 1.96.
  • Higher confidence → larger z* → wider interval. Only a bigger n buys that width back.

Rules of the game for step 4

  • Split the leftover area in TWO. A 90% interval leaves 10%, which is 5% in each tail — not 10% in one.
  • z* is a lookup, not a calculation. The only arithmetic is deciding what tail area to look up.
  • z* has no units and no n in it. It depends on the confidence level and nothing else.

Say it to a friend

“z* is just how many standard errors wide I need to go to capture the middle 90 or 95 percent of the bell curve. It’s a lookup, not a calculation.”

Key insight

Everything that changes between a 90% and a 99% interval is this one number. The estimate, the standard error and the data are all identical — which is why confidence level is a decision, not a finding.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Using the one-tail area where the two-tail one belongs. For 90% the answer is 1.645, from 5% in each tail — looking up 10% in one tail gives 1.28, which is the 80% value.

🪙 The sampling distribution of a proportion

The same three facts — but now the spread depends on p itself.

⬇ builds onshape, centre and spread from step 2⬆ hands up√(p(1−p)/n) — the standard error every proportion formula is built on
  • ▸CENTRE. The mean of the sampling distribution is p — the sample proportion is unbiased.
  • ▸SPREAD. σp̂ = √(p(1−p)/n). No separate σ is needed: p and n decide it between them.
  • ▸SHAPE. As n increases the sampling distribution becomes approximately Normal.
🪝 memory hookp(1−p) is a seesaw — highest in the middle, tipping to nothing at either end. Uncertainty is greatest when the population is genuinely split.

In words

Switch from means to proportions and the three facts survive intact. The centre is p, so p̂ is unbiased. The shape becomes approximately Normal as n grows. And the spread is √(p(1−p)/n) — notice there is no separate σ to be given, because for a proportion the spread is decided entirely by p and n.

  • Spread is largest at p = 0.5 and shrinks towards the extremes — a fact step 9 uses to choose sample sizes.
  • Near 0 or 1 the Normal shape fails, because the distribution is squashed against a boundary. That is what the successes-and-failures conditions guard against.

Rules of the game for step 5

  • For a proportion the mean and the spread are not independent. p decides both, which is why you never get given a σ.
  • p(1−p) is largest at 0.5 and falls away symmetrically. A genuinely split population is the hardest one to pin down.
  • The Normal approximation is an approximation. It is good in the middle, poor at the edges, and the counts conditions are how you check which you are in.

Say it to a friend

“A sample percentage jumps around from sample to sample, and how much it jumps has a formula: the square root of p times one minus p, over n. Bigger samples, steadier percentages.”

Key insight

A proportion is a mean in disguise — the mean of a column of 0s and 1s. That is why the three facts transfer unchanged, and why √(p(1−p)/n) is just σ ⁄ √n with the right σ substituted.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Forgetting the square root. p(1−p)/n is a VARIANCE; the standard error is its root. With p̂ = 0.426 and n = 251 that is the difference between 0.00097 and 0.0312.

For p = 0.5 and n = 100, what is the standard error? And for p = 0.1?
√(0.5 × 0.5 ⁄ 100) = √0.0025 = 0.05. For p = 0.1: √(0.1 × 0.9 ⁄ 100) = √0.0009 = 0.03. The split population is noticeably noisier, which is exactly the seesaw.
Why does the Normal approximation break down when p is very close to 0?
Because p̂ cannot go below zero, so the distribution is squashed against that boundary and becomes strongly skewed — nothing like a symmetric bell. The successes-and-failures conditions are a practical way of asking whether you are far enough from the wall.

🫙 The interval for a single proportion

The same method as for means, with one substitution — and one condition to state.

⬇ builds onthe standard error of p̂ and a critical value z*⬆ hands upa finished interval, and a verdict on somebody’s claim
  • ▸Same method as for means: estimate ± z* × standard error. Only the standard error changes.
  • ▸SE of a sample proportion = √(p̂(1−p̂)/n), using the sample value in place of the unknown p.
  • ▸State the conditions: an SRS, and successes and failures BOTH at least 10.
🪝 memory hookp-hat, z-star, root p-hat q-hat over n. Say it as one phrase; it is the only formula in 8.1 you must be able to write cold.

In words

Everything is now in place. Take the estimate p̂, multiply the standard error by z*, and put it either side. The only new thing is that the standard error uses p̂ in place of the unknown p — and that this substitution needs a condition: an SRS, with successes and failures both at least 10.

  • The beads: 107 red out of 251 → p̂ = 0.426; SE = 0.0312; m = 1.645 × 0.0312 = 0.051; interval (0.375, 0.477).

Rules of the game for step 6

  • The condition is about COUNTS, not percentages and not n alone. 400 people with only 6 successes fails it however large n is.
  • An interval that excludes the claimed value is evidence against that claim — which makes the interval almost a test you can read off directly.
  • Report the interval, then interpret it in the words of the study. A pair of numbers with no sentence earns no marks.

➕ Four imaginary observations

A one-line repair for an interval that quietly over-promises.

⬇ builds onthe large-sample interval, and the knowledge that it is approximate⬆ hands upan interval that keeps the confidence level it advertises
  • ▸The problem: the large-sample interval’s actual confidence level is usually LESS than the level you asked for when you chose z*.
  • ▸The fix: add four imaginary observations — two successes and two failures.
  • ▸p̃ = (X + 2) ⁄ (n + 4), and then the same interval formula with n + 4 underneath.
🪝 memory hookTwo in, two out — invite two imaginary successes and two imaginary failures to the party, and the interval starts behaving.

In words

The large-sample interval is easy to understand because it comes straight from the approximate Normal distribution of p̂. The trouble is that its TRUE confidence level is usually below the one you asked for. The repair is almost absurdly simple: pretend you saw four more observations, two successes and two failures, and run exactly the same formula.

  • Its effect is to pull the estimate towards 0.5, which widens intervals that were too narrow near the edges.
  • It matters most in small samples. At n = 1 500 it moves nothing; at n = 20 it changes the answer.

Rules of the game for step 7

  • Both the numerator and the denominator change. p̃ = (X + 2)/(n + 4), and the standard error uses n + 4 as well — using n there is the usual slip.
  • Plus-four is for INTERVALS. The significance test has its own standard error, built from p₀, and does not use it.
  • The four are imaginary, so they never go in your reported counts. They are a correction to the method, not data.
  • For TWO proportions the plus-four is different: one imaginary success and one imaginary failure added to EACH sample, so p̃ᵢ = (Xᵢ + 1)/(nᵢ + 2), with n₁ + 2 and n₂ + 2 underneath.

Say it to a friend

“The normal formula is a bit over-confident with small samples — it gives intervals that are too narrow. The trick is to pretend you saw four extra people, two of each kind, and then do exactly the same calculation.”

Key insight

Adding two of each kind is a nudge towards 0.5, which is where the standard error is largest. That is the whole mechanism: it widens exactly the intervals that were too narrow, and leaves large samples alone.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Adding 2 to the numerator and forgetting the 4 in the denominator. With 3 successes out of 20 that gives 5 ⁄ 20 = 0.25 instead of 5 ⁄ 24 = 0.208 — and an interval built on a number that is not a proportion of anything.

🥔 Testing a claim about one proportion

The other direction of travel: fix a value, and ask how strange the data would be.

⬇ builds onthe sampling distribution of p̂, and a specific claimed value p₀⬆ hands upa P-value and a decision about somebody’s claim
  • ▸The hypotheses: H₀: p = p₀ against a one- or two-sided Hₐ. For the potatoes, H₀: p = 0.08 against Hₐ: p > 0.08.
  • ▸The statistic: z = (p̂ − p₀) ÷ √(p₀(1−p₀)/n) — the distance from the claim, in standard errors.
  • ▸The standard error uses p₀, NOT p̂, because the test asks what would happen if H₀ were true.
🪝 memory hookIn the null world, use the null’s numbers. The test borrows p₀ for its standard error; the interval has no claim to borrow from, so it uses p̂.

In words

An interval reaches outward from the data. A test does the opposite: it fixes a claimed value p₀, builds the sampling distribution that would hold IF that claim were true, and measures how far your result landed from the centre of it. The one detail worth memorising is that the standard error uses p₀, not p̂ — because the whole calculation happens inside the null world.

  • The P-value is the probability of a z this large or larger in the direction Hₐ specifies.
  • The decision: p̂ = 47/500 = 0.094 gives z = 1.15 and P = 0.1251 > α = 0.10, so we fail to reject H₀ and the truckload is used.
📌 A note worth having

Rules of the game for step 8

  • In the null world, use the null’s numbers. The test borrows p₀ for its standard error; the interval, having no claim to borrow from, uses p̂.
  • Check the conditions with p₀ too: np₀ and n(1−p₀) both at least 10. For the potatoes that is 40 and 460.
  • “Fail to reject” never means “H₀ is true”. It means this sample was not strange enough — and a bigger sample might well have been.

Say it to a friend

“I assume the claim is true, work out how much a sample percentage would wobble in that world, and count how many wobbles away my result landed. If that’s a common distance, the claim survives.”

Key insight

The potato verdict is about the sample, not the shipment. 9.4% against a claimed 8% in a sample of 500 is ordinary; the same 9.4% in a sample of 5 000 would be strong evidence. Significance is a statement about evidence, not about size.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Using p̂ in the test’s standard error. That is the interval’s formula, and it is the single most common error in this chapter — the two look almost identical on the page.

📊 How many people do you need?

Run the margin-of-error formula backwards, and make one safe guess.

⬇ builds onthe margin of error, and the fact that p(1−p) peaks at 0.5⬆ hands upa sample size you can defend — and a worked end-to-end example
  • ▸The formula: n = (z* ⁄ m)² × p*(1−p*), where p* is a guessed value for the sample proportion.
  • ▸Why a guess is needed: the margin of error involves p̂, which you do not have before you collect the data.
  • ▸The safe guess is p* = 0.5, because p(1−p) is largest there — so the margin will always be at most m.
🪝 memory hookp* = 0.5 is the insurance policy. It costs you a few extra respondents and guarantees you never under-deliver on the margin you promised.

In words

Everything so far has started from data. This step starts from a promise: “our margin of error is no more than three points”. Rearrange m = z*√(p̂(1−p̂)/n) for n and you get n = (z*/m)² × p*(1−p*). The awkwardness is that you need p̂ before you have collected anything — so you guess, and the safe guess is 0.5.

  • Always round UP. 1 067.1 becomes 1 068 respondents, because rounding down would leave the margin above what you promised.
  • Halving m quadruples n. The m sits squared in the denominator, so precision gets expensive fast.

Rules of the game for step 9

  • Round UP, always, even from 1 067.1 to 1 068. Sample size is the one place where rounding to the nearest whole number is wrong.
  • p* = 0.5 is the worst case, so it is the safe one. A smaller guess saves respondents but risks a wider margin than you advertised.
  • z* and m must match the promise. A 90% promise uses 1.645; using 1.96 silently buys a confidence level nobody asked for and a bill to match.

Say it to a friend

“I decide how precise I want to be, look up z* for my confidence level, and the formula tells me how many people to survey. If I’ve no idea what the percentage will be, I assume 50% because that’s the hardest case.”

Key insight

The whole lecture meets here. Plan the sample size, collect the data, estimate with an interval, test a specific claim — the spring-break question walks all four in order, and every proportion question in this course is some subset of them.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Rounding 1 067.1 down to 1 067. The whole point of the calculation was to guarantee a margin no larger than m, and 1 067 respondents leaves it fractionally larger.

⚖️ Two groups, two proportions

Everything doubles — but the three facts stay the same three facts.

⬇ builds onthe whole one-sample machine, steps 2 to 9⬆ hands upan interval for the GAP between two groups
  • ▸The statistic: p̂₁ − p̂₂ is our best guess for p₁ − p₂, and its centre is p₁ − p₂.
  • ▸The spread: √(p̂₁(1−p̂₁)/n₁ + p̂₂(1−p̂₂)/n₂). The two VARIANCES add, because each sample brings its own uncertainty.
  • ▸The interval: (p̂₁ − p̂₂) ± z* × SE, with z* from the confidence level exactly as before.
🪝 memory hookVariances add, standard errors don’t. Square, add, root — in that order, every single time you combine two sources of noise. And for an interval the two groups keep their own money: each contributes its own p̂.

In words

The second half of the lecture compares two groups: two populations, or two treatments in a randomised experiment. The statistic is now p̂₁ − p̂₂, and the three facts survive again. Its centre is p₁ − p₂, so it is unbiased. Its shape is approximately Normal for large samples. And its spread is built by adding the two VARIANCES and then taking the root.

  • The conditions: two random samples or two groups in a randomised experiment, and all four counts — n₁p̂₁, n₁(1−p̂₁), n₂p̂₂, n₂(1−p̂₂) — at least 10.
  • Teens and adults: 0.73 − 0.47 = 0.26 ± 1.96 × 0.0189 → (0.223, 0.297). Entirely above zero, so there is good evidence the two groups differ.

Rules of the game for step 10

  • Variances add; standard errors do not. Square, add, root — in that order, every time you combine two sources of noise.
  • For an INTERVAL each group keeps its own p̂. Pooling belongs to the test in step 11, and only there.
  • Independence is essential. Two separate samples, never paired or repeated measures on the same people.

Say it to a friend

“I subtract the two percentages to get the gap, work out how much that gap wobbles by combining both samples’ wobble, and put z* times that either side. If the range doesn’t include zero, the two groups really do differ.”

Key insight

An interval for a difference is CLOSE to a two-sided test you can read off: if it excludes zero, the test will almost always reject H₀: p₁ = p₂. Not identical, though — the interval is unpooled and the test is pooled, so near the boundary they can disagree. The interval’s advantage is that it also says how big the gap is.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading the interval as a range for one group. (0.223, 0.297) is a range for the GAP — teens minus adults, in percentage points — not a range for the proportion of teens who use social media.

🥣 Pooling, and why only here

The breakfast study — and the single most examinable distinction in the chapter.

⬇ builds onthe two-sample standard error from step 10⬆ hands upa P-value for H₀: p₁ = p₂, and a rule about which SE goes where
  • ▸Why the test pools: if H₀: p₁ = p₂ is true, the two parameters are the SAME number, so it makes sense to combine the data to estimate it.
  • ▸The pooled proportion: p̂ = (successes in both samples) ÷ (individuals in both samples) = 45 ⁄ 230 = 0.1957.
  • ▸The statistic: z = (p̂₁ − p̂₂) ÷ √(p̂(1−p̂)(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)) = 0.0642 ÷ 0.0549 = 1.17.
🪝 memory hookThe test believes H₀; the interval does not. Believing it means the two groups share one proportion, so you pool. Not believing it means each group keeps its own.

In words

For the test, H₀ says the two proportions are EQUAL. If that is true then both samples are estimating the same number, so the sensible estimate of it combines them: the pooled proportion, all successes over all individuals. That is the only difference between the test and the interval, and it is the distinction the exam asks about.

  • The P-value, two-sided: 2P(z ≥ 1.17) = 2(1 − 0.8790) = 0.2420. Greater than α = 0.05, so we fail to reject H₀.
  • The conclusion: not sufficient evidence that the proportions skipping breakfast differ between the two schools.
📌 A note worth having
On the P-value: the slide reads 0.2420 from Table A after rounding z to 1.17. The unrounded z is 1.1683, whose exact two-sided P is 0.2427. Same decision either way — the gap is table rounding, not a different test.

Rules of the game for step 11

  • The test believes H₀; the interval does not. Believing it means the two groups share one proportion, so you pool. Not believing it means each group keeps its own p̂.
  • The pooled proportion is all successes over all individuals — NOT the average of the two sample proportions. 45 ⁄ 230 = 0.1957, while the average of 0.2375 and 0.1733 is 0.2054.
  • Match the tails to Hₐ. A two-sided alternative doubles the tail area; a one-sided one does not.
  • The conditions here are the deck’s own, and they are LOOSER than for the interval: all four counts at least 5 for the two-sample test, against at least 10 for the two-sample interval. Plus independence of the two samples.

Say it to a friend

“For the test I pretend both groups have the same true percentage, so I lump all the successes together to estimate it. For the interval I’m not assuming that, so I keep them separate.”

Key insight

Pooling is not an approximation or a convenience — it is the null hypothesis expressed as arithmetic. The moment you stop assuming p₁ = p₂, pooling stops being justified, which is exactly why the interval never uses it.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Averaging the two sample proportions to pool. The larger sample has to count for more: 19 and 26 successes out of 80 and 150 people pool to 45 ⁄ 230 = 0.1957, not to (0.2375 + 0.1733) ⁄ 2 = 0.2054.

➗ Ratios, and the last question

The third way to compare two proportions — and why a doubled risk can still be nothing.

⬇ builds ontwo sample proportions you already know how to compute⬆ hands upa second way to report the comparison, and a caution about reading it
  • ▸Relative risk is the RATIO of two proportions rather than their difference.
  • ▸RR = 1 means the two proportions are equal — the null value for a ratio is one, not zero.
  • ▸The study: 683 of 3 220 women with a first birth at 30 or later were “really happy” (21.2%); 1 498 of 10 245 with an earlier first birth (14.6%). RR = 0.212 ⁄ 0.146 = 1.45.
🪝 memory hookDifference has a null of 0; ratio has a null of 1. If you remember nothing else about relative risk, remember which number means “no effect”.

In words

There are two ways to compare two proportions: subtract them, or divide them. Dividing gives the relative risk, and the thing to hold on to is that its null value is 1, not 0. A relative risk of 1.45 sounds dramatic; whether it is depends entirely on the two proportions it came from, which is why you always report them too.

  • Say it carefully: women with a late first child have 1.45 times the “risk” of being really happy — an association in an observational study, not advice about when to have children.
  • Confidence intervals for RR are more complicated and need software, but they rest on the same principles.

Rules of the game for step 12

  • Difference has a null of 0; ratio has a null of 1. If you remember nothing else about relative risk, remember which number means “no effect”.
  • A ratio always needs its two proportions reported alongside. Doubling a risk of 1 in a million and doubling a risk of 1 in 3 are the same ratio and entirely different news.
  • This is an observational study. “1.45 times the risk” is an association, and the word “risk” here is a technical term, not a warning.

Say it to a friend

“You can compare two percentages by subtracting them or by dividing them. Dividing gives a relative risk — 1.45 times as likely. It sounds bigger than it is unless you also say what the two percentages actually were.”

Key insight

The same two proportions give a difference of 6.6 percentage points and a ratio of 1.45. Neither is more correct; they answer different questions, and quoting only the one that sounds larger is how statistics gets its reputation.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading RR = 1.45 as “45% of women” or as a difference. It is a multiplier: 21.2% is 1.45 times 14.6%. The difference between those two proportions is 6.6 percentage points, which is a different number answering a different question.

Check yourself

21.2% of one group and 14.6% of the other were “really happy”. Give the difference and the relative risk, and say what each means.
Difference: 21.2 − 14.6 = 6.6 percentage points more in the late-first-birth group. Relative risk: 0.212 ⁄ 0.146 = 1.45, so that group is 1.45 times as likely. The null value is 0 for the difference and 1 for the ratio — and both describe the same data.
A drug halves the risk of a condition. Why is that not enough information?
Because a ratio says nothing about the size of the risks it compares. Halving a risk from 2 in a million to 1 in a million is the same RR = 0.5 as halving a risk from 40% to 20%, and only one of those is worth taking a drug for. Always report the two proportions alongside the ratio.

  • c.2.576
  • d.1.645
A 90% interval leaves 10% out, which is 5% in each tail, giving z* = 1.645.
The other options
✗a. 1.96 is the 95% value.
✗b. 1.28 is the 80% value — it comes from leaving 10% in ONE tail rather than in each.
✗c. 2.576 is the 99% value.
🏷️ Split the leftover area in two before you look it up. That one habit prevents the 1.28 mistake.
Question 5 · step 5 · the spread of p̂
Which expression is the standard error of a sample proportion?
  • a.√( p̂(1 − p̂) ) ⁄ n
  • b.√( p̂(1 − p̂) ⁄ n )
  • c.p̂(1 − p̂) ⁄ n
  • d.√( p̂ ⁄ n )
The variance of p̂ is p(1−p)/n, so the standard error is its square root, with p̂ substituted for the unknown p.
The other options
✗a. The n has escaped from under the root — it must be inside it, dividing the variance before the root is taken.
✗c. That is the VARIANCE. Forgetting the square root is the commonest slip in this chapter.
✗d. The (1 − p̂) has gone missing, so the spread no longer peaks at 0.5.
🏷️ Variance first, then root. p(1−p)/n is a variance; its square root is the standard error.
Question 6 · step 6 · the interval for p
107 of 251 beads are red. Which is the correct 90% confidence interval for p?
  • a.(0.375, 0.477)
  • b.(0.395, 0.457)
  • c.(0.365, 0.487)
  • d.(0.426, 0.477)
p̂ = 0.426; SE = √(0.426 × 0.574 ⁄ 251) = 0.0312; m = 1.645 × 0.0312 = 0.051, giving 0.426 ± 0.051.
The other options
✗b. That margin is about 0.031 — the standard error itself, with the z* multiplication forgotten.
✗c. That is the 95% interval, built with z* = 1.96 instead of 1.645.
✗d. The margin has only been added, never subtracted — the lower end is still p̂.
🏷️ Estimate, standard error, multiply by z*, then BOTH directions. Four moves, in order.
Question 7 · step 6 · the interval for p
When may you use the large-sample z interval for a single proportion?
  • a.When the sample size n is at least 30, whatever the counts turn out to be
  • b.When the population the sample was drawn from is Normally distributed
  • c.When the sample proportion p̂ falls somewhere between 0.4 and 0.6
  • d.When the numbers of successes and failures in the sample are both at least 10
The condition is about COUNTS, not percentages and not n alone: both successes and failures must reach 10. A sample of 400 with only 6 successes fails it, and plus-four is the fix.
The other options
✗a. No n threshold appears anywhere in this chapter — 400 people with 6 successes still fails.
✗b. The population here is a yes/no variable, so it is never Normal. It is the sampling distribution that becomes approximately Normal.
✗c. No such range is stated, and it would rule out perfectly usable large samples near the edges.
🏷️ Count the successes and the failures — at least 10 for every interval and for the one-sample test. The two-sample test is the one place the deck allows 5.
Question 8 · step 7 · plus four
A sample has 3 successes out of 20. What is the plus-four estimate p̃?
  • a.0.250
  • b.0.120
  • c.0.208
  • d.0.150
p̃ = (X + 2) ⁄ (n + 4) = 5 ⁄ 24 = 0.208. Adding two successes and two failures pulls the estimate towards 0.5 and stops the interval being unrealistically narrow.
The other options
✗a. 0.250 adds 2 to the numerator but forgets the 4 in the denominator: 5 ⁄ 20.
✗b. 0.120 adds 4 to the denominator but forgets the 2 on top: 3 ⁄ 25.
✗d. 0.150 is the raw p̂ = 3 ⁄ 20, with no correction applied at all.
🏷️ Two successes AND two failures: +2 on top, +4 underneath. Both halves of the fix.
Question 9 · step 8 · testing a claim
Which is the correct test statistic for H₀: p = p₀?
  • a.z = (p̂ − p₀) ⁄ √( p₀(1 − p₀)/n )
  • b.z = (p̂ − p₀) ⁄ √( p̂(1 − p̂)/n )
  • c.z = (p̂ − p₀) ⁄ (p₀/n)
  • d.z = (p̂ − p₀) × √n
The test works inside the null world, so the standard error uses p₀.
The other options
✗b. That is the INTERVAL’s standard error. Using it in a test is the single most common error in this chapter.
✗c. That is not a standard error at all — no (1 − p₀), and no square root.
✗d. The spread of p̂ is missing entirely; multiplying by √n is not the same as dividing by a standard error.
🏷️ In the null world, use the null’s numbers. p₀ for the test, p̂ for the interval.
Question 10 · step 8 · testing a claim
The potato test gives z = 1.15 and P = 0.1251, with α = 0.10. What is the conclusion?
  • a.Reject H₀: the shipment does contain more than 8% blemished potatoes
  • b.Reject H₀, because the test statistic z came out greater than 1
  • c.The test is invalid here, because the sample proportion exceeds p₀
  • d.Fail to reject H₀: not sufficient evidence that more than 8% are blemished
0.1251 is greater than α = 0.10, so H₀ survives and the producer uses the truckload. This does not prove the shipment is at 8% — with a larger sample the same 9.4% would have been significant.
The other options
✗a. The P-value is above α, so there is not enough evidence to reject.
✗b. z > 1 means nothing on its own. The bar at α = 0.10 for a one-sided test is z = 1.28.
✗c. p̂ exceeding p₀ is exactly what makes the test worth running; it does not invalidate it.
🏷️ Compare P with α, never z with 1. And “fail to reject” is never “H₀ is true”.
Question 11 · step 8 · testing a claim
A P-value of 0.03 means:
  • a.There is a 97% probability that the alternative hypothesis is true
  • b.3% of the sample contradicts H₀
  • c.If H₀ were true, results at least this extreme would occur in 3% of samples
  • d.There is a 3% probability that H₀ is true

The other options
✗a. Same error, written as its complement — the alternative gets no probability either.
✗b. A P-value is about how extreme the whole result is, not about a fraction of individual observations.
✗d. The hypothesis never receives a probability. It is either true or it is not.

Question 12 · step 9 · how many people
How many people must you survey to estimate a proportion within 0.03 at 95% confidence?
  • a.2 135
  • b.1 068
  • c.1 067
  • d.385
n = (1.96 ⁄ 0.03)² × 0.5(1 − 0.5) = 1 067.1, and sample size is always rounded UP.
The other options
✗a. 2 135 drops the (1 − p*): it multiplies by p* = 0.5 instead of by p*(1 − p*) = 0.25, which doubles n.
✗c. 1 067 is the unrounded figure truncated — it would leave the margin above the promised 0.03.
✗d. 385 comes from a pilot value of p* = 0.1 instead of the safe 0.5 — or from setting the margin at 0.05 rather than 0.03. The two happen to give the same 384.16.

Question 13 · step 9 · how many people
Why is p* = 0.5 the safe guess in the sample-size formula?
  • a.Because most proportions met in practice turn out to be close to 0.5
  • b.Because it makes the arithmetic in the formula simpler to compute
  • c.Because the Normal approximation only works for values near 0.5

✗b. It does simplify the arithmetic, but that is a side effect, not the reason.

Question 14 · step 9 · how many people
812 of 1 500 students say they go to Florida. Testing H₀: p = 0.50 against p > 0.50 gives z = 3.20. What follows?
  • a.The test is invalid, because p̂ is so close to 0.5
  • b.P = 0.0007, so reject H₀: a majority do go
  • c.P = 0.0007, so fail to reject H₀
  • d.P = 0.0014, because the test is two-sided

Question 15 · step 10 · two groups
What is the standard error of the difference between two independent sample proportions?
  • a.√( p̂₁(1−p̂₁)/n₁ + p̂₂(1−p̂₂)/n₂ )
  • b.√( p̂₁(1−p̂₁)/n₁ ) − √( p̂₂(1−p̂₂)/n₂ )
  • c.√( p̂₁(1−p̂₁)/n₁ ) + √( p̂₂(1−p̂₂)/n₂ )
  • d.√( (p̂₁ − p̂₂)(1 − p̂₁ + p̂₂)/(n₁ + n₂) )
Variances add, then you take the root — square, add, root.
The other options
✗b. Subtracting standard errors would say that comparing two noisy numbers is LESS uncertain than either one, which is nonsense.
✗c. Standard errors do not add; variances do. This overstates the spread.
✗d. That is an invented formula — it treats the difference of two proportions as though it were itself a proportion.
🏷️ Square, add, root. In that order, every time you combine two sources of noise.
Question 16 · step 10 · two groups
A 95% interval for the difference in social-media use is (0.223, 0.297) for teens minus adults. What does it say?
  • a.95% of teens use the sites more often than 22.3% of adults do
  • b.The difference between the two groups is not statistically significant
  • c.More teens than adults use the sites, by between 22.3 and 29.7 percentage points
  • d.Between 22.3% and 29.7% of all teens use social-networking sites
The interval is about the DIFFERENCE, not about either proportion — and because it lies entirely above zero, there is good evidence the two groups differ.
The other options
✗a. That is not a statement anything in this chapter produces.
✗b. An interval lying entirely above zero is exactly what significance looks like.
✗d. That reads it as a range for one group. It is a range for the GAP; 73% of teens use the sites.

Question 17 · step 11 · pooling

  • a.0.2375
  • b.0.1957
  • c.0.2054
  • d.0.1733
(19 + 26) ⁄ (80 + 150) = 45 ⁄ 230 = 0.1957 — all successes over all individuals.

✗c. 0.2054 is the simple AVERAGE of the two sample proportions, which ignores that the second school is nearly twice the size.

Question 18 · step 11 · pooling

  • a.The pooled one, using all successes over all individuals

  • c.Whichever of the two comes out smaller
  • d.The unpooled one, using each group’s own p̂
Pooling assumes p₁ = p₂, which is the null hypothesis — fine for the test, wrong for an interval that is trying to estimate how big the difference is.

✗c. Choosing a standard error by its size is not a statistical argument at all.
